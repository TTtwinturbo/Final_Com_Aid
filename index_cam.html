6.1 · Week 6

The Trapezoidal Rule

The definite integral is the area under the curve. When the function has no antiderivative — or exists only as a table of measurements — approximate the curve with straight lines and add up the trapezoids.

Why integrate numerically at all?

Polynomials, $\ln x$ and a few others integrate in closed form. Many integrals that matter in engineering do not. The slides give two: the integral behind the error function, and the complete elliptic integral of the first kind (which sets the exact period of a large-swing pendulum — the same pendulum that returns in Chapter 7):

$$ \int_a^b e^{-x^2}\,dx \qquad\qquad K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}} $$

Neither has an elementary antiderivative; their values were historically looked up in tables. And when $f$ comes from a sensor, there is no formula at all — only samples $f(x_0), f(x_1),\dots$. Numerical integration handles both cases the same way.

One trapezoid

$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ means: multiply $f$ at each $x$ by a sliver $dx$ and sum from $a$ to $b$ — the area under the curve. Replace $f$ with the straight line through $\big(a,f(a)\big)$ and $\big(b,f(b)\big)$, and that area is a trapezoid:

1 Trapezoidal rule (single segment)
$$ I=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\approx\; (b-a)\,\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} \;=\; \frac{h}{2}\big[f(x_0)+f(x_1)\big],\qquad h=b-a $$
Width × average height. The line is a first-order (linear) Lagrange polynomial, so the rule is exact for any straight line and wrong for anything that curves.

Composite trapezoidal rule

One trapezoid is crude, so split $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal segments of width $h=(b-a)/n$, with nodes $x_i=x_0+ih$, and apply the rule to each. Every interior point is shared by two neighbouring trapezoids, so it is counted twice:

2 Composite trapezoidal rule
$$ I\approx\frac{h}{2}\Big[f(x_0)+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(x_i)+f(x_n)\Big],\qquad h=\frac{b-a}{n} $$
Pattern of weights: $1,2,2,\dots,2,1$ times $h/2$. Works for any $n\ge1$ and for unequally spaced data too, if you sum individual trapezoids.

With $n=4$ ($h=0.5$) on the slide cubic: $f = 1,\;1.5,\;1,\;1,\;3$ at $x=0,\,0.5,\,1,\,1.5,\,2$, so

$$ I\approx\frac{0.5}{2}\big[1+2(1.5+1+1)+3\big]=0.25(11)=2.75,\qquad \varepsilon_t=-3.1\% $$

Quick check

A composite trapezoid with $n=10$ gives $\varepsilon_t=-0.5\%$. Roughly what error do you expect with $n=20$?

Why: the error is $O(h^2)$. Doubling $n$ halves $h$, so the error falls by $2^2=4$: $-0.5\%/4=-0.125\%$. (Option B would be an $O(h^4)$ method — Simpson.)

6.2 · Week 6

Simpson's Rules & Newton–Cotes

Straight lines can't follow curvature. A parabola through three points can — and a lucky symmetry makes Simpson's rule exact for cubics too, so it jumps two orders of accuracy for the price of one.

Simpson's 1/3 rule: integrate a parabola

Take three equally spaced points $x_0=a$, $x_1=a+h$, $x_2=b$ with $h=(b-a)/2$, fit the second-order Lagrange polynomial through them, and integrate that polynomial exactly. A quick way to see the weights: shift so the points sit at $-h,0,h$ and write the parabola as $p(x)=A+Bx+Cx^2$.

$$ \int_{-h}^{h}p\,dx = 2Ah+\tfrac{2}{3}Ch^3,\qquad A=f_1,\quad C=\frac{f_0-2f_1+f_2}{2h^2} $$ $$ \Rightarrow\quad \int_{-h}^{h}p\,dx = 2hf_1+\frac{h}{3}\big(f_0-2f_1+f_2\big)=\frac{h}{3}\big(f_0+4f_1+f_2\big) $$

The odd term $Bx$ integrates to zero over the symmetric interval — the same symmetry kills $x^3$, which is why Simpson's rule is exact for cubics even though it was built from a parabola.

3 Simpson's 1/3 rule (two segments)
$$ I\approx\frac{h}{3}\big[f(x_0)+4f(x_1)+f(x_2)\big]=(b-a)\,\frac{f(x_0)+4f(x_1)+f(x_2)}{6},\qquad h=\frac{b-a}{2} $$
Still width × weighted-average height — the weights $1:4:1$ (out of 6) favour the midpoint.

Composite Simpson's rule

Chain parabolas over consecutive pairs of segments — so $n$ must be even. Odd-indexed points are parabola midpoints (weight 4); even interior points are shared joints (weight 2):

4 Composite Simpson's 1/3 rule ($n$ even)
$$ I\approx\frac{h}{3}\Big[f(x_0)+4\!\!\sum_{i=1,3,5}^{n-1}\!\! f(x_i)+2\!\!\sum_{j=2,4,6}^{n-2}\!\! f(x_j)+f(x_n)\Big],\qquad h=\frac{b-a}{n} $$
Weights: $1,4,2,4,2,\dots,4,1$ times $h/3$. Error $\approx-\frac{(b-a)h^4}{180}\bar f^{(4)}$ (standard result, not on the slides): halving $h$ cuts it by 16.

Simpson's 3/8 rule

Fit a cubic through four points ($h=(b-a)/3$) and integrate it:

5 Simpson's 3/8 rule (three segments)
$$ I\approx\frac{3h}{8}\big[f(x_0)+3f(x_1)+3f(x_2)+f(x_3)\big]=(b-a)\,\frac{f_0+3f_1+3f_2+f_3}{8},\qquad h=\frac{b-a}{3} $$
Same order as the 1/3 rule (exact up to cubics) but needs one more point. Its practical use: when $n$ is odd, apply 3/8 to the last three segments and 1/3 to the remaining even number.

MATLAB — Composite Simpson 1/3simpsonComposite.m 
function I = simpsonComposite(f, a, b, n)
% Composite Simpson's 1/3 rule; n must be even
if mod(n,2) ~= 0, error('n must be even'); end
h = (b - a)/n;
s = f(a) + f(b);
for i = 1:n-1
    if mod(i,2) == 1
        s = s + 4*f(a + i*h);   % odd interior points
    else
        s = s + 2*f(a + i*h);   % even interior points
    end
end
I = h/3 * s;
end
Quick check

You have 7 equally spaced data points (6 segments) of a measured force and want the work $\int F\,dx$. Which is the most accurate standard choice?

Why: both Simpson options are fourth order, but for the same $h$ the composite 1/3 error is $\approx\tfrac{(b-a)h^4}{180}|\bar f^{(4)}|$ against $\tfrac{(b-a)h^4}{80}|\bar f^{(4)}|$ for 3/8 — about 2.25× smaller (standard results, not on the slides). Gauss Quadrature

Newton–Cotes fixes the sample points (equally spaced) and only chooses weights. Gauss lets the method choose the points too — doubling the free parameters and nearly doubling the polynomial degree it integrates exactly.

The idea

Every rule so far has the form $I\approx\sum_i W_i\,f(x_i)$. The trapezoid uses $x_1=a,\ x_2=b$ with $W_1=W_2=\frac{b-a}{2}$. With $n$ points there are $2n$ unknowns ($n$ weights, $n$ locations), so we can demand exactness for $2n$ basis polynomials $1,\xi,\xi^2,\dots,\xi^{2n-1}$. Work on the standard interval $\xi\in[-1,1]$ and map afterwards.

Deriving the 2-point rule

Require $W_1 f(\xi_1)+W_2 f(\xi_2)=\int_{-1}^{1}f\,d\xi$ for $f=1,\ \xi,\ \xi^2,\ \xi^3$:

$$ \begin{aligned} W_1+W_2&=\textstyle\int_{-1}^1 1\,d\xi=2 & W_1\xi_1+W_2\xi_2&=\textstyle\int_{-1}^1\xi\,d\xi=0\\ W_1\xi_1^2+W_2\xi_2^2&=\textstyle\int_{-1}^1\xi^2 d\xi=\tfrac23 & W_1\xi_1^3+W_2\xi_2^3&=\textstyle\int_{-1}^1\xi^3 d\xi=0 \end{aligned} $$

Symmetry suggests $W_1=W_2$ and $\xi_1=-\xi_2$; the first equation gives $W_1=W_2=1$, the third gives $2\xi_2^2=\tfrac23$:

6 Two-point Gauss–Legendre rule
$$ \int_{-1}^{1}f(\xi)\,d\xi\approx f\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right)+f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right),\qquad \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}=0.5773502692 $$
Two function evaluations, exact for every cubic — the same exactness Simpson needs three evaluations for.

The 3-point rule follows the same way (six equations): $\xi=0,\ \pm\sqrt{0.6}$ with weights $\tfrac89,\ \tfrac59$, exact through degree 5:

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}f\,d\xi\approx\tfrac59 f\!\left(-\sqrt{0.6}\right)+\tfrac89 f(0)+\tfrac59 f\!\left(\sqrt{0.6}\right) $$

MATLAB — Gauss–Legendre, 1–3 pointsgaussQuad.m 
function I = gaussQuad(f, a, b, n)
% Gauss-Legendre quadrature, n = 1, 2 or 3 points
switch n
    case 1, xi = 0;                        W = 2;
    case 2, xi = [-1 1]/sqrt(3);           W = [1 1];
    case 3, xi = [-sqrt(0.6) 0 sqrt(0.6)]; W = [5 8 5]/9;
end
I = 0;
for i = 1:n
    x = (a + b)/2 + (b - a)/2 * xi(i);   % map xi -> x
    I = I + W(i) * f(x);
end
I = (b - a)/2 * I;                       % dx = (b-a)/2 dxi
end
Quick check

A 3-point Gauss rule is exact for polynomials up to which degree?

Why: $n$ points give $2n$ free parameters, so exactness up to degree $2n-1=5$. A 3-point Newton–Cotes rule (Simpson) only reaches degree 3 because its points are fixed.

Numerical Differentiation

A derivative is the limit of a slope between two points. Stop before the limit — use a small but finite $h$ — and the Taylor series tells you exactly how wrong you are. These formulas are the building blocks of every PDE scheme in Chapter 8.

$$ \frac{dy}{dx}=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x} $$

Forward, backward, central

Expand $f(x_{i+1})$ in a Taylor series about $x_i$, with $h=x_{i+1}-x_i$:

$$ f(x_{i+1})=f(x_i)+f'(x_i)\,h+\frac{f''(x_i)}{2!}h^2+\frac{f'''(x_i)}{3!}h^3+\cdots $$

Solve for $f'(x_i)$: everything from the $h^2$ term onward, divided by $h$, becomes an error that starts at $\frac{f''}{2}h$ — first order. Doing the same backwards with $f(x_{i-1})$ gives the backward difference. Subtracting the two expansions cancels the $f''$ terms, leaving an error that starts at $h^2$:

8 First-derivative difference formulas
$$ \underbrace{f'(x_i)=\frac{f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i)}{h}+O(h)}_{\text{forward}}\qquad \underbrace{f'(x_i)=\frac{f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})}{h}+O(h)}_{\text{backward}} $$ $$ \underbrace{f'(x_i)=\frac{f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i-1})}{2h}+O(h^2)}_{\text{central}} $$
Central uses the same two-point cost as the others but is one order more accurate, because the symmetric stencil cancels the even Taylor terms.

Adding the two expansions instead cancels the odd terms ($f'$ and $f'''$) and isolates $f''$:

$$ f(x_{i+1})+f(x_{i-1})=2f(x_i)+f''(x_i)\,h^2+O(h^4) $$
9 Second central difference
$$ f''(x_i)=\frac{f(x_{i+1})-2f(x_i)+f(x_{i-1})}{h^2}+O(h^2) $$
Weights $1,-2,1$. This exact stencil reappears as $\partial^2T/\partial x^2$ in every Chapter 8 scheme — memorise it here.

Chapter 7

Ordinary Differential Equations

An ODE tells you the slope everywhere; an initial condition tells you where to start. Every method in this chapter marches forward with the same update — new value = old value + slope × step — and differs only in how cleverly it estimates that slope.

7.1 · Week 6

Initial-Value Problems

The slides open with a swinging pendulum because it shows both reasons we need numerical ODE solvers: real equations are nonlinear, and higher-order equations have to be rewritten as first-order systems before any method can touch them.

The swinging pendulum

A mass $m$ on a light rod of length $L$, angle $\theta$ from vertical. Newton's second law tangentially gives a second-order ODE, which the slides immediately split into two first-order ODEs by naming the angular velocity $\omega=d\theta/dt$:

$$ \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{d\theta}{dt}=\omega,\qquad \frac{d\omega}{dt}=-\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta $$

Linear vs nonlinear

For $a\,\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}+b\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}+c\,y=d$: the equation is linear if $a,b,c$ are constants or functions of $x$ only — exact solutions are then usually easy. It is nonlinear if a coefficient depends on $y$ itself; exact solutions generally don't exist. The pendulum is nonlinear because

$$ \sin\theta=\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\frac{\theta^7}{7!}+\cdots $$

contains powers of the unknown. For small swings, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and the equation linearises to $\ddot\theta+\frac{g}{L}\theta=0$, with exact solution (for $\theta(0)=\theta_0$, $\dot\theta(0)=0$)

$$ \theta(t)=\theta_0\cos\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{g}{L}}\;t\right) $$

That formula is only an approximation of the real pendulum — Section 7.5 solves the nonlinear one numerically and lets you see exactly when the approximation breaks.

The general first-order IVP

1 Initial-value problem
$$ \frac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y),\qquad y(x_0)=y_0 $$
When $f$ depends only on $x$, this is just integration (Chapter 6). The slide example: $f=3x^2-2x+1$ integrates to $y=x^3-x^2+x+C$, and $y(0)=2$ fixes $C=2$. The difficulty — and the reason for this chapter — is when $f$ depends on $y$, the very thing you don't know yet.
2 Every one-step method in this chapter
$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+\phi\,h,\qquad h=x_{i+1}-x_i $$
$\phi$ is an estimate of the average slope over the step. Euler: slope at the start. Heun: average of start and (predicted) end. Modified Euler: slope at the midpoint. RK4: a weighted blend of four slopes.

See the slope field

$f(x,y)$ assigns a slope to every point of the plane. The running example of the slides is $dy/dx=y\cos x$, whose exact solution through $y(0)=1$ is $y=e^{\sin x}$ (separate variables: $\int dy/y=\int\cos x\,dx$). Click anywhere to start a solution curve there.

Every solution curve is tangent to the little segments everywhere. A numerical method only ever sees the segments — it has to follow them in finite steps, and every step drifts a little off the true curve.

7.2 · Week 6

Euler's Method

Stand at the current point, read the slope there, walk a straight line for one step, repeat. Simple, cheap — and first-order accurate, which is why everything after it exists.

Replace the derivative by a forward difference (Section 6.4) at $x_i$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx}\approx\frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{x_{i+1}-x_i}=\frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{h}=f(x_i,y_i) $$
3 Euler's method
$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+f(x_i,y_i)\,h $$
From the Taylor series $y_{i+1}=y_i+y_i'h+\frac{y_i''}{2}h^2+\cdots$, Euler keeps only the first two terms: each step makes a local error $\sim\frac{y''}{2}h^2$. Over a fixed interval you take $\sim1/h$ steps, so the global error is $O(h)$ — halve $h$, halve the error.

7.3 · Week 6

Heun's Method & Modified Euler

Euler's flaw is using the slope at the start for the whole step. Two fixes, both second order: average the start and end slopes (Heun), or use the slope at the middle (modified Euler).

Heun's method — predictor–corrector

We'd like the slope at the end of the step, $f(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})$, but $y_{i+1}$ is what we're computing. So predict it with Euler, use the prediction to estimate the end slope, then correct with the average:

4 Heun's method
$$ \text{Predictor: } y^0_{i+1}=y_i+f(x_i,y_i)\,h \qquad\quad \text{Corrector: } y_{i+1}=y_i+\frac{f(x_i,y_i)+f(x_{i+1},y^0_{i+1})}{2}\,h $$
Two $f$-evaluations per step; global error $O(h^2)$. It is the ODE version of the trapezoidal rule — and identical to it when $f$ doesn't depend on $y$.

Modified Euler — the midpoint method

Walk half a step with Euler, read the slope there, and use that midpoint slope for the full step from $x_i$:

5 Modified Euler (midpoint) method
$$ y_{i+1/2}=y_i+f(x_i,y_i)\frac{h}{2},\qquad y'_{i+1/2}=f\!\left(x_{i+1/2},\,y_{i+1/2}\right),\qquad y_{i+1}=y_i+f\!\left(x_{i+1/2},y_{i+1/2}\right)h $$
Also two evaluations, also $O(h^2)$. It is the ODE cousin of the midpoint integration rule.

7.4 · Week 7

Runge–Kutta Methods

Runge–Kutta methods match the Taylor series of the true solution to higher and higher order without ever computing a derivative of $f$ — only extra evaluations of $f$ at cleverly chosen points.

General form

6 n-th order Runge–Kutta
$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+\phi(x_i,y_i,h)\,h,\qquad \phi=a_1k_1+a_2k_2+\cdots+a_nk_n $$ $$ \begin{aligned} k_1&=f(x_i,\,y_i)\\ k_2&=f(x_i+p_1h,\;y_i+q_{11}k_1h)\\ k_3&=f(x_i+p_2h,\;y_i+q_{21}k_1h+q_{22}k_2h)\\ &\;\;\vdots \end{aligned} $$
$\phi$ is the "increment function" — the average slope over the step. Each $k$ depends on the ones before it, so they are computed in order. The constants $a,p,q$ are chosen to match the Taylor series.

Deriving second order (where Heun and midpoint come from)

With $n=2$: $y_{i+1}=y_i+(a_1k_1+a_2k_2)h$. Target: the three-term Taylor series, using the chain rule for $f'=\frac{df}{dx}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+f(x_i,y_i)\,h+\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}f\right)\frac{h^2}{2}\qquad\text{(A)} $$

Expand $k_2$ with a two-variable Taylor series, $g(x+r,y+s)\approx g+r\,\partial_xg+s\,\partial_yg$:

$$ k_2\approx f+p_1h\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+q_{11}k_1h\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\;\Rightarrow\; y_{i+1}=y_i+(a_1+a_2)f\,h+\left(a_2p_1\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+a_2q_{11}f\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)h^2\qquad\text{(B)} $$

Matching (A) and (B) term by term:

7 Second-order RK conditions
$$ a_1+a_2=1,\qquad a_2\,p_1=\tfrac12,\qquad a_2\,q_{11}=\tfrac12 $$
Three equations, four unknowns → a one-parameter family. Pick $a_2$: $a_2=\tfrac12\Rightarrow a_1=\tfrac12,\ p_1=q_{11}=1$: Heun ($k_2$ = slope at the predicted end). $a_2=1\Rightarrow a_1=0,\ p_1=q_{11}=\tfrac12$: modified Euler ($k_2$ = slope at the midpoint).

Third and fourth order

8 Third-order RK (slides)
$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+\tfrac16\big(k_1+4k_2+k_3\big)h $$ $$ k_1=f(x_i,y_i),\quad k_2=f\!\left(x_i+\tfrac12h,\;y_i+\tfrac12k_1h\right),\quad k_3=f\!\left(x_i+h,\;y_i-k_1h+2k_2h\right) $$
Weights $1:4:1$ — Simpson's rule in disguise.
9 Classical fourth-order RK
$$ y_{i+1}=y_i+\tfrac16\big(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4\big)h $$ $$ \begin{aligned} k_1&=f(x_i,\;y_i) & k_2&=f\!\left(x_i+\tfrac12h,\;y_i+\tfrac12k_1h\right)\\ k_3&=f\!\left(x_i+\tfrac12h,\;y_i+\tfrac12k_2h\right) & k_4&=f(x_i+h,\;y_i+k_3h) \end{aligned} $$
Start slope, two midpoint slopes (the second refined with the first), end slope; weights $1:2:2:1$. Four evaluations per step, global error $O(h^4)$. Use the "Anatomy of one step" visual in 7.3 (choose RK4) to see where each $k$ is sampled.

        case 'heun'
            y0p    = yi + f(xi, yi)*h;                          % predictor
            y(i+1) = yi + (f(xi, yi) + f(xi + h, y0p))/2 * h;   % corrector
        case 'midpoint'   % modified Euler
            ym     = yi + f(xi, yi)*h/2;
            y(i+1) = yi + f(xi + h/2, ym)*h;
        case 'rk4'
            k1 = f(xi,       yi);
            k2 = f(xi + h/2, yi + k1*h/2);
            k3 = f(xi + h/2, yi + k2*h/2);
            k4 = f(xi + h,   yi + k3*h);
            y(i+1) = yi + (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)*h/6;
    end
    x(i+1) = xi + h;
end
end

8.1 · Week 7

Classifying PDEs & Boundary Conditions

Before choosing a method, identify the type. The type decides whether information travels everywhere at once, diffuses forward in time, or propagates as waves — and that decides how the numerical scheme must be built.

Definitions

A PDE has a dependent variable that depends on two or more independent variables, e.g. $u(x,y)$. The order is the highest derivative that appears, so $\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=0$ is second order.

  • Linear: integer-order derivatives and coefficients that do not depend on $u$ — e.g. $\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}+7u=12$. Coefficients may depend on $x,y$.
  • Nonlinear: a power or product of $u$ or its derivatives — e.g. $\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)^{0.5}=0$, or $u\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ terms. Closed-form solutions generally don't exist; numerical methods are the only route.
1 Classification of second-order linear PDEs
$$ a\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+b\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x\,\partial y}+c\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=f\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad b^2-4ac\;\begin{cases}\lt0 & \text{elliptic}\\=0 & \text{parabolic}\\\gt0 & \text{hyperbolic}\end{cases} $$
Same test as classifying a conic $ax^2+bxy+cy^2$ — ellipse, parabola, hyperbola. Only the second-derivative coefficients matter; first-order terms and $f$ don't change the type.

Elliptic Equations — Steady Heat in a Plate

The syllabus names the elliptic equation explicitly for Chapter 8. The finite-difference version is beautifully simple: every interior temperature is the average of its four neighbours.

Where the Laplace equation comes from

Take a small element $\Delta x\times\Delta y$ of a plate of thickness $t$ and conductivity $k$. At steady state, heat in = heat out:

$$ q_x+q_y-q_{x+\Delta x}-q_{y+\Delta y}=0,\qquad q_x=-k\,(t\,\Delta y)\frac{\partial T}{\partial x},\quad q_y=-k\,(t\,\Delta x)\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} $$

Expand $q_{x+\Delta x}\approx q_x+\frac{\partial q_x}{\partial x}\Delta x$ (Taylor series), divide by $t\,\Delta x\,\Delta y$, and let $\Delta x,\Delta y\to0$:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(k\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(k\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right)=0 $$
2 Laplace and Poisson equations (constant k)
$$ \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}=0\quad\text{(Laplace)}\qquad\qquad \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}=f(x,y)\quad\text{(Poisson, with surface heating)} $$

The five-point stencil

Apply the second central difference (Section 6.4, formula 9) in each direction at grid node $(i,j)$:

$$ \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}\approx\frac{T_{i+1,j}-2T_{i,j}+T_{i-1,j}}{(\Delta x)^2},\qquad \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}\approx\frac{T_{i,j+1}-2T_{i,j}+T_{i,j-1}}{(\Delta y)^2} $$
3 Finite-difference Laplace equation ($\Delta x=\Delta y$)
$$ T_{i+1,j}+T_{i-1,j}+T_{i,j+1}+T_{i,j-1}-4T_{i,j}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad T_{i,j}=\frac{T_{i-1,j}+T_{i+1,j}+T_{i,j-1}+T_{i,j+1}}{4} $$
Error $O(\Delta x^2)$. One equation per unknown node → a sparse linear system (Chapter 3). The averaging form is exactly the Gauss–Seidel update, so you can also just sweep it until it stops changing. A consequence worth remembering: no interior node can be hotter than the hottest boundary or colder than the coldest.

Parabolic Equations — Explicit Scheme

Transient heat conduction in a rod. The explicit scheme computes each new temperature directly from three old ones — easy to code, but only stable if the time step is small enough.

The heat equation

Element of a rod with cross-section $A$, density $\rho$, specific heat $c$: heat in − heat out = heat stored,

$$ q_x-q_{x+\Delta x}=\rho\,c\,A\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t},\qquad q_x=-kA\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} $$

Taylor-expand $q_{x+\Delta x}$, divide by $A\,\Delta x$, let $\Delta x\to0$; for constant $k$:

4 One-dimensional heat equation
$$ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\frac{k}{\rho c}\,\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2} $$
$k/\rho c$ is the thermal diffusivity (often written $\alpha$), units m²/s.

Forward in time, central in space

Superscript $n$ = time level, subscript $i$ = grid point. Use a forward difference in time ($O(\Delta t)$) and the second central difference in space ($O(\Delta x^2)$), both at the known time level $n$:

$$ \frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}=\frac{k}{\rho c}\,\frac{T_{i+1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} $$
5 Explicit finite difference
$$ T_i^{n+1}=T_i^n+\lambda\left(T_{i+1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i-1}^n\right),\qquad \lambda=\frac{k\,\Delta t}{\rho c\,(\Delta x)^2} $$
Stable only if $\lambda\le\tfrac12$, i.e. $\displaystyle\Delta t\le\frac{\rho c\,(\Delta x)^2}{2k}$. Halving $\Delta x$ forces a 4× smaller $\Delta t$ — 8× more work overall.
Why exactly ½? Rewrite the update as a weighted average $$T_i^{n+1}=\lambda\,T_{i-1}^n+(1-2\lambda)\,T_i^n+\lambda\,T_{i+1}^n$$ The three weights always sum to 1. While $\lambda\le\frac12$ they are all non-negative, so the new temperature is a genuine average of the old ones and can never overshoot them — physically right for diffusion. Once $\lambda\gt\frac12$ the centre weight goes negative: a node that is slightly hotter than its neighbours becomes colder than them next step, and the zig-zag grows by a factor up to $|1-4\lambda|$ each step. At $\lambda=1$ that factor is 3.

Implicit & Crank–Nicolson Schemes

Evaluate the spatial derivative at the new time level and the stability limit disappears. The price: each step is now a tridiagonal linear system instead of a direct formula.

Fully implicit (backward in time)

Same forward difference in time, but the second derivative is taken at time $n+1$:

$$ \frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}=\frac{k}{\rho c}\,\frac{T_{i+1}^{n+1}-2T_i^{n+1}+T_{i-1}^{n+1}}{(\Delta x)^2} $$
6 Implicit finite difference
$$ -\lambda\,T_{i-1}^{n+1}+(1+2\lambda)\,T_i^{n+1}-\lambda\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}=T_i^n $$
Three unknowns per equation → a tridiagonal system for all interior nodes at each step. Unconditionally stable; still $O(\Delta t)$ in time, so large steps are stable but not necessarily accurate.

Crank–Nicolson

Average the explicit and implicit spatial derivatives — centring the scheme at $t^{n+1/2}$ makes it second order in time as well:

$$ \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}\approx\frac12\left[\frac{T_{i+1}^{n}-2T_i^{n}+T_{i-1}^{n}}{(\Delta x)^2}+\frac{T_{i+1}^{n+1}-2T_i^{n+1}+T_{i-1}^{n+1}}{(\Delta x)^2}\right] $$
7 Crank–Nicolson
$$ -\lambda\,T_{i-1}^{n+1}+2(1+\lambda)\,T_i^{n+1}-\lambda\,T_{i+1}^{n+1}=\lambda\,T_{i-1}^n+2(1-\lambda)\,T_i^n+\lambda\,T_{i+1}^n $$
Tridiagonal, unconditionally stable, $O(\Delta t^2,\Delta x^2)$. Usually the best accuracy per unit of work for parabolic problems.

Solving the tridiagonal system: the Thomas algorithm

Gauss elimination (Chapter 3) on a tridiagonal matrix only ever touches the three diagonals, so it costs $O(n)$ instead of $O(n^3)$. With sub-diagonal $a_i$, diagonal $b_i$, super-diagonal $c_i$ and right side $d_i$:

$$ \text{forward: } m=\frac{a_i}{b_{i-1}},\quad b_i\leftarrow b_i-m\,c_{i-1},\quad d_i\leftarrow d_i-m\,d_{i-1}\qquad \text{back: } x_n=\frac{d_n}{b_n},\quad x_i=\frac{d_i-c_i\,x_{i+1}}{b_i} $$
MATLAB — Thomas algorithm (no backslash)thomas.m 
function x = thomas(a, b, c, d)
% Tridiagonal solve: a = sub-diag, b = diag, c = super-diag, d = rhs
n = numel(d);
for i = 2:n                       % forward elimination
    m    = a(i)/b(i-1);
    b(i) = b(i) - m*c(i-1);
    d(i) = d(i) - m*d(i-1);
end
x = zeros(n,1);
x(n) = d(n)/b(n);                 % back substitution
for i = n-1:-1:1
    x(i) = (d(i) - c(i)*x(i+1))/b(i);
end
end

function T = heatImplicitCN(T, lambda, nSteps, scheme)
% scheme = 'implicit' (BTCS) or 'cn' (Crank-Nicolson); Dirichlet ends
n = numel(T); m = n - 2;                 % number of interior unknowns
for s = 1:nSteps
    a = -lambda*ones(m,1); c = -lambda*ones(m,1);
    if strcmp(scheme, 'implicit')
        b = (1 + 2*lambda)*ones(m,1);
        d = T(2:n-1); d = d(:);
    else
        b = 2*(1 + lambda)*ones(m,1);
        d = zeros(m,1);
        for i = 2:n-1
            d(i-1) = lambda*T(i-1) + 2*(1 - lambda)*T(i) + lambda*T(i+1);
        end
    end
    d(1) = d(1) + lambda*T(1);           % known boundary values
    d(m) = d(m) + lambda*T(n);           % move to the right-hand side
    T(2:n-1) = thomas(a, b, c, d);
end

Hyperbolic Equations — Vibrating String

A plucked string obeys the wave equation. The explicit scheme uses three time levels, needs a special first step, and its stability limit has a beautiful physical meaning: the numerical grid must be able to keep up with the wave.

The wave equation

A string under constant tension $T$, weight per unit length $w$, deflection $u(x,t)$. Newton's second law on a segment $\Delta x$ whose ends make angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ with the horizontal:

$$ T\sin\beta-T\sin\alpha=\frac{w}{g}\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} $$

For small angles $\sin\approx\tan=$ slope, so $T\left[\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right|_{x+\Delta x}-\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right|_{x}\right]=\frac wg\Delta x\,u_{tt}$; divide by $\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x\to0$:

8 Wave equation
$$ \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2}=k^2\,\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2},\qquad k^2=\frac{Tg}{w} $$
$k$ is the wave speed along the string.

Explicit scheme

Central differences in both $t$ and $x$:

$$ \frac{u_i^{n+1}-2u_i^n+u_i^{n-1}}{(\Delta t)^2}=k^2\,\frac{u_{i+1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} $$
9 Explicit wave scheme
$$ u_i^{n+1}=2u_i^n-u_i^{n-1}+C\left(u_{i+1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i-1}^n\right),\qquad C=\frac{k^2(\Delta t)^2}{(\Delta x)^2} $$
The slides call $C$ the Courant number: $C\lt1$ converges, $C\gt1$ diverges, and $C=1$ gives maximum efficiency (largest step) but is marked "not recommended" — it sits exactly on the stability boundary, leaving no margin if, say, the true wave speed is slightly higher than assumed. Many textbooks define the Courant number as $k\Delta t/\Delta x$ — the square root of this $C$; the stability boundary (=1) is the same either way. Physical reading: in one step the wave travels $k\Delta t$, which must not exceed one grid spacing $\Delta x$.

Mixed quiz

1 · Ch 6

Simpson's 1/3 rule is built from a parabola, yet integrates cubics exactly. Why?

Why: the cubic's difference from the interpolating parabola is $\propto(x-x_0)(x-x_1)(x-x_2)$, which is antisymmetric about $x_1$, so it integrates to zero over $[x_0,x_2]$.
2 · Ch 8

The explicit heat scheme with $\Delta x=0.1$ and $k/\rho c=1$ is run with $\Delta t=0.004$. Stable?

Why: $\lambda=0.004/0.01=0.4\le0.5$. A small λ is never a stability problem — just more steps.
3 · Ch 7

Halving $h$ in RK4 should reduce the global error by about…

Why: global $O(h^4)$ → $2^4=16$. (32× would be the local $O(h^5)$ error of a single step.)
4 · Ch 6

Map $\int_2^6 f(x)\,dx$ onto $[-1,1]$. Which is correct?

Why: $x=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}\xi=4+2\xi$. Check: $\xi=-1\to x=2$, $\xi=1\to x=6$.
5 · Ch 8

For the Laplace equation on a grid with 7 × 3 unknown interior nodes, the linear system has how many equations?

Why: boundary nodes are known (Dirichlet), so only the 21 interior nodes get an equation — the slide's sine-plate example.
6 · Ch 7

The second-order RK family has one free parameter because…

Why: $a_1+a_2=1$, $a_2p_1=\frac12$, $a_2q_{11}=\frac12$ with unknowns $a_1,a_2,p_1,q_{11}$.
7 · Ch 8

The wave scheme with $C=1$ reproduces the slide's exact solution to every digit. That means it is…

Why: with $C=1$ the update $u_i^{n+1}=u_{i+1}^n+u_{i-1}^n-u_i^{n-1}$ is exactly d'Alembert's solution sampled on the grid. For $C\lt1$ the scheme is stable but only second order (try $C=0.5$ in 8.5 and watch the ripples).
8 · Ch 6 → 8

Which Chapter 6 formula is inside every Chapter 8 scheme on this page?

Why: Laplace, explicit, implicit, Crank–Nicolson and the wave scheme all discretise $\partial^2/\partial x^2$ with $1,-2,1$. The forward difference appears in the heat schemes' time derivative, but not in Laplace or the wave scheme.
